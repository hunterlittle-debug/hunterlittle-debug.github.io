Overview

The problem

NC State SolarPack is a student team designing and building a street-legal, solar-powered car to compete in the Formula Sun Grand Prix (FSGP). The car's high-voltage battery pack has to be safely contained, so the battery box is one of the most safety-critical parts of the vehicle.

On the Structures team I helped design that battery box, including choosing firewall materials that meet the competition's required safety standards. I also updated the team's outdated structural models in SolidWorks so they match the current vehicle design, and helped assemble the battery modules themselves.

Right now the build is paused while parts arrive and the team heads into winter break. Work picks back up in the spring.

Goals & requirements

  • Contain the high-voltage pack and isolate it from the driver with a compliant firewall
  • Meet the Formula Sun Grand Prix (FSGP) regulations for battery enclosures
  • Fit within the current vehicle structure (matching the updated SolidWorks models)
  • Assemble cells into series-connected high-voltage modules
  • Protect the driver first: the box is designed to keep a fire or thermal event away from the cockpit, not to cool the cells
  • Keep heat away from the car's sensitive electronics

Engineering decisions

Design choices

The key decisions I made, the alternatives I weighed, and why I picked what I did.

01

Firewall material selection

Selected firewall materials for the battery box to meet the required safety standards.

Why: We compared epoxies and resin layups with fire-retardant additives, then welding blankets, and landed on ceramic blankets sandwiched between outer layers (final layer materials still being decided). Ceramic blanket gave the best fire protection for the driver while still letting us meet the Formula Sun Grand Prix (FSGP) regulations.

02

Series module configuration

Cells are wired in series into high-voltage modules.

Why: Wiring lithium-ion cells in series adds their voltages together, building up to the roughly 400 V pack the car's drive system runs on.

03

Spot-welded nickel and copper strips

Joined cells with spot-welded nickel and copper strips instead of soldering.

Why: Spot welding puts very little heat into the cell, which protects it, and makes strong, low-resistance joints that hold up to vibration on the road.

What I learned

Working on a real competition car taught me how to step into creative roles when requirements change. Plans shifted and budgets were very tight, and we still had to meet the race regulations and get the job done. On the Business team I also reach out to companies for sponsorships, which has taught me how to explain our engineering to people outside the team.
